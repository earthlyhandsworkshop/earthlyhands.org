Mary Caroline Atkinson

ScreenplayView artworkProduction notes

Muskogee, Indian Territory — October 23, 1900

A hearing already in progress.

No establishing speech. No explanation of the law.

A stenographer, MYRA YOUNG, takes the words down.

Across from the Commission sits MARY CAROLINE ATKINSON, thirty-eight.

The surviving transcript does not name the individual examiner.

So neither do we.

COMMISSION

What is your name?

MARY

Mary Caroline Atkinson.

COMMISSION

What is your age?

MARY

I am 38.

COMMISSION

What is your post-office address?

MARY

Durant, Indian Territory.

Question.

Answer.

Question.

Answer.

The room finds its rhythm.

COMMISSION

Where did you live prior to your residence in the Indian Territory?

MARY

Pontotoc County, Mississippi, in the town of Pontotoc.

COMMISSION

How long did you live in Mississippi?

MARY

I was born and raised there.

COMMISSION

How much Choctaw blood do you claim?

Mary considers it.

MARY

I suppose about one sixth.

Myra records.

COMMISSION

What is your father’s name?

MARY

Robert Bell.

The name lands without music.

COMMISSION

Is your father living?

MARY

No sir.

COMMISSION

What is your mother’s name?

MARY

Elizabeth D. Bell.

COMMISSION

Through which one of your parents do you claim your Choctaw blood?

MARY

My father.

COMMISSION

How long has your father been dead?

MARY

He’s been dead four years.

COMMISSION

Was his name ever on any of the tribal rolls of the Choctaw Nation?

MARY

Yes sir it was on the roll of the Mississippi Choctaws, the treaty of 1830. It was registered in Mississippi.

COMMISSION

How old was your father when he died?

MARY

82.

COMMISSION

You state that your father was a member of the Choctaw tribe of Indians in 1830 and was enrolled by the Choctaw authorities at that time?

MARY

His name is on the register.

COMMISSION

Have you any evidence of the fact that he was a recognized member of the Choctaw tribe of Indians at that time?

MARY

Of course I don’t know anything only what I have heard them say. His name is there and I have often heard him speak of his right here.

Myra records.

COMMISSION

When did your father come to the Indian Territory?

MARY

When I did, five years ago.

COMMISSION

Did he make application to the Choctaw tribal authorities in the Indian Territory for citizenship in the Choctaw Nation?

MARY

He never did. He was sick when he come and did not live very long after we moved here.

A beat.

The questioning continues.

Mary has never applied before.

Her name is not on the tribal roll in the Commission’s possession.

She was not an 1896 applicant.

She was not admitted by United States court judgment.

This is her first application.

COMMISSION

You are now making application for identification as a Mississippi Choctaw?

MARY

Yes sir.

COMMISSION

Will you state plainly to the Commission the ground upon which you make this claim?

MARY

Well, my father’s name was on the roll and that is the ground that we have for citizenship that our father was part Choctaw.

COMMISSION

What evidence have you of the fact that your father was a recognized and enrolled member of the Choctaw tribe of Indians?

MARY

His name is there on the roll.

COMMISSION

On what roll?

MARY

I don’t know what they call it. Mississippi Choctaws. The treaty of 1830. I don’t know what they call it.

A brief silence.

The Commission has a record.

Mary has Robert Bell.

The Commission reads from its record.

COMMISSION

No party by the name of Robert Bell appears upon the approved roll of locations of the Choctaw reservations under the fourteenth article of the treaty of 1830 approved by the President of the United States June 3rd, 1836.

Nothing moves.

ROBERT BELL.

Mary is still sitting there.

COMMISSION

Mrs. Atkinson, there is no record of your father having been on the roll of citizens of the Choctaw Nation who elected to remain in Mississippi under the 14th article of the treaty of 1830 and who received and claimed land there.

MARY

He didn’t receive any land there.

COMMISSION

Do you claim under the provisions of the 14th article of the treaty of 1830?

MARY

That is when my father registered there.

COMMISSION

Do you make your claim under the provisions of the 14th article of the treaty of 1830?

MARY

Yes sir.

COMMISSION

Did any of your ancestors ever comply with the provisions of the 14th article of the treaty of 1830?

MARY

I don’t know.

COMMISSION

Did any of your ancestors ever receive or claim any land in Mississippi as beneficiaries under the 14th article of the treaty of 1830?

MARY

Not that I know of.

The Commission explains its authority.

Article Fourteen.

Documents.

Compliance.

Proof.

The words accumulate around Mary.

COMMISSION

Have you any documentary evidence showing that any of your ancestors ever complied with the provisions of that treaty either in receiving or claiming any lands in Mississippi as beneficiaries thereunder?

MARY

I don’t think that my father ever received any land there or was ever benefitted by the lands there. If he was I—we always had a home there and lived there until we come out here. I know that he was very anxious for his family all to come to the Indian Territory.

COMMISSION

How did your father acquire possession of the land that he held in Mississippi?

MARY

He bought it.

COMMISSION

He did not receive it as a grant from the United States government as a member of the Choctaw tribe of Indians?

MARY

No sir, we did not live in that reservation. I know there was a reservation where a great many Indians lived but there wasn’t any in the county where we lived. It was in the lower counties, further south from where we were.

COMMISSION

Did you or your father ever live in the Choctaw reservation in Mississippi?

MARY

I don’t know. I don’t think we ever did. Grand mother’s people did.

The Commission keeps narrowing.

COMMISSION

Do you make any claim by reason of any of the other provisions of the treaty of 1830?

MARY

I want a claim, if I am not accounted a Mississippi Choctaw I want to be recognized as a Choctaw because my grand mother was.

COMMISSION

What I am trying to find out from you is on what ground you are basing your claim.

The Commission gathers her answers together.

Rolls.

Citizenship.

Application.

Mississippi.

Land.

Then—

COMMISSION

You state that your father never received any land in Mississippi—

Mary stops it.

MARY

I said not that I knew of.

Silence.

Myra records that too.

COMMISSION

And the Commission is unable to identify your father, whom you claim to have been on the 1830 roll in Mississippi as being there, and I want to know if you make your claim on any other treaty stipulations?

MARY

I don’t know much about the treaties. I have never read anything about them and of course I don’t know very much about it only what I have heard my ancestors say.

COMMISSION

Is there anything further you want to say in regard to your claim? Any material facts that you want to present to the Commission?

MARY

Nothing more only that my father was a part Choctaw. That is all the grounds I have. There may be a whole lot more but I don’t know what it is. Of course I don’t want to say anything only what I know to be so.

COMMISSION

Are you married?

MARY

Yes sir.

COMMISSION

What is your husband’s name?

MARY

Forest Atkinson.

Six children.

Don Ellis.

Cammie Elizabeth.

Robert Pratt.

Sallie Bell.

The twins, Leonard and Lottie.

Four months old.

Their claims are identical with Mary’s.

Papers are offered.

The original petition.

Affidavits.

EXHIBIT A.

Identified.

Filed.

Made part of the record.

The examination is finished.

COMMISSION

The decision on your application and those of your six minor children will be mailed to you in writing in the near future to your present post-office address.

Mary rises.

A small, ordinary leave-taking.

Nothing ceremonial.

Then she goes.

Her footsteps.

The door opens.

Mary passes through.

The door closes.

BOOM.

BLACK.

Silence.

A date appears against the black.

APRIL 15 1902

REFUSED.

Hold it only long enough to read.

Gone.

BLACK.

TWO FAMILIES,
ONE ROBERT

Why this scene works

Mary first. The machinery arrives around her.

Enter mid-hearing

No historical lecture opens the film. Question and answer establish the room before the audience is told what the case is supposed to mean.

Let the name land

“Robert Bell” arrives as Mary’s father before it becomes a disputed administrative object. The person precedes the government’s file.

Keep knowledge honest

Mary’s limits are not weakness to repair. Her refusal to say more than she knows is part of her authority in the scene.

Article Fourteen is pressure

The legal category enters through the Commission’s narrowing questions. The film does not need a narrator to explain the treaty before Mary encounters its administrative use.

The correction matters

When the Commission strengthens “not that I know of” into “never received,” Mary stops the move. That tiny correction lets the audience hear the difference between testimony and characterization.

Leave ordinarily

The hearing ends as ordinary procedure. The door / BOOM / REFUSED sequence is film grammar, not a claim that the surviving record preserves the literal sound of her exit.

Source brake
